Borda counts change when a slate runs more candidates

The same voters, with the same loyalties, can win a different number of seats under the Borda count just because one slate runs more or fewer candidates. Scottish STV holds steady. These charts show how big the swing is, in a 5-seat and a 50-seat election.

The test election
  • 1,000 voters elect 5 seats.100 voters elect 50 seats.
  • Slate A has 40% of voters. Its fair share is 2 seats (40% of 5)20 seats (40% of 50).
  • Slate B has 60% of voters and always runs 550 candidates.
  • Slate A runs anywhere from 1 to 1210 to 120 candidates. Nothing else changes: running more candidates splits a slate's support among them, but doesn't win it new supporters.
  • Each point is 1,000 simulated elections. Hover or tap a point to see how often slate A won its fair share.

The Borda count gives a candidate points for each ballot: the more candidates are running, the more points a first choice is worth, down to 0 for anyone left off. The highest totals win. Approval here counts every candidate a voter ranked as approved, and the most approved win.

Chart 1 · Disciplined slates, everyone ranks every candidate

Under Borda, slate A wins more seats the more candidates it runs

Each extra candidate from slate A lands between A's favorites and slate B on every A voter's ballot. That widens the points gap between A's favorites and B's candidates on every A ballot. On B's ballots, the extra candidate lands below everyone who matters, so it costs A nothing there. Running 12 candidates instead of 2 nearly doubles slate A's seats, from 0.8 to 1.6, with no change in what voters want. STV stays at exactly 2 seats however many candidates A runs. Running 120 candidates instead of 20 nearly doubles slate A's seats, from 9 to 17, with no change in what voters want. STV stays at 20 seats however many candidates A runs. Approval is left out of this chart: if every voter ranks everyone, every candidate is approved by every voter.

Chart 2 · Disciplined slates, each voter ranks 550 candidates

With shorter ballots, the same extra candidates push slate A out

When voters rank only as many candidates as there are seats, each extra candidate on the field raises the points every ranked candidate gets, for every voter. Slate B has more voters, so it gains more from each raise. Once slate A runs 8 candidates, it wins nothing under Borda. Under Borda, slate A falls from 8 seats when it runs 10 candidates to under 1 seat when it runs 100. The direction of the effect flips with ballot length, which is part of the problem: a slate can't know in advance whether running more helps or hurts. Approval gives the majority slate every seatnearly every seat here, whatever slate A does.

Chart 3 · Loose slates, each voter ranks 550 candidates

With loose slates, every method rewards a slate that runs about as many candidates as it can win

Here voters lean toward their own slate (4 times as likely to pick it at each rank) but often rank the other slate's candidates too, closer to how members vote in real organizations. Every method peaks when slate A runs 3 or 4 candidates. Running more spreads A's votes too thin. Under STV the loss is gradual, from 2.1 to 1.4 seats at 12 candidates, as votes leak to slate B and ballots run out. Under Borda and approval, slate A drops to under 1 seat. Every method peaks when slate A runs 30 to 40 candidates. Running more spreads A's votes too thin. Under STV the loss is gradual, from 22 to 21 seats at 120 candidates. Under Borda and approval, slate A drops from 22 seats to 9.

What this shows and doesn't. These elections use two slates and a simplified voter (each voter picks one candidate at a time, favoring their slate and popular candidates). They show how each counting method reacts to slate size. They don't predict a real election's numbers.

With 100 voters and 50 seats, a candidate needs only 2 votes to win under STV, so single votes move seats. The STV lines wobble a little for that reason.
