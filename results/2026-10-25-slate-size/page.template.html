Why a Borda count rewards or punishes a slate for how many candidates it runs

Under the Borda count, the same voters can elect a different number of a slate's candidates depending only on how many candidates the slate puts up. Two tiny elections show why, and simulations show how big the effect gets.

How a Borda count works

Each ballot hands out points by rank. If N candidates are running, a voter's first choice gets N − 1 points, their second choice N − 2, and so on down to 0. Candidates a voter leaves off get 0. The candidates with the most points win the seats.

The key detail: a first choice is worth more points when more candidates are running. So every candidate who enters the race changes the value of every rank on every ballot.

Example 1 · Everyone ranks every candidate

An extra candidate nobody needed wins slate A the seat

One seat. Slate A has 2 voters, slate B has 3. Everyone ranks their own slate first, then the rest.

Slate A runs 1 candidate

VotersBallotA1B1
2 slate AA1, B12 × 10
3 slate BB1, A103 × 1
Points23 ✓

B1 wins, as you'd expect: more voters prefer B1.

Slate A adds a second candidate

VotersBallotA1A2B1
2 slate AA1, A2, B12 × 22 × 10
3 slate BB1, A1, A23 × 103 × 2
Points7 ✓26

Now A1 wins. Nobody changed their mind about A1 or B1.

What happened: on slate A's ballots, A2 sits between A1 and B1, so A1's lead over B1 grows from 1 point to 2 on each of those ballots. On slate B's ballots, A2 lands at the bottom, below A1, so B1's lead over A1 stays at 1 point. So slate A gained ground on its own ballots and lost none on slate B's. The extra candidate worked as a spacer.

Simulation 1 · Disciplined slates, everyone ranks every candidate

The spacer effect at scale: more candidates, more seats

This switch changes all three simulations.

1,000 voters elect 5 seats. Slate A has 40% of voters, so its fair share is 2 seats. Slate B has 60% and runs 5 candidates. Slate A runs 1 to 12. 100 voters elect 50 seats. Slate A has 40% of voters, so its fair share is 20 seats. Slate B has 60% and runs 50 candidates. Slate A runs 10 to 120. Running more candidates splits a slate's support among them but doesn't win it new voters. Each point is the average of 1,000 simulated elections.

Under Borda, slate A goes from 0.8 seats with 2 candidates to 1.6 seats with 12. Under STV it wins exactly 2 seats however many it runs. Under Borda, slate A goes from 9 seats with 20 candidates to 17 seats with 120. Under STV it wins about 20 seats however many it runs. Slate B could fight back by running spacers of its own, so a Borda election rewards whichever slate floods the ballot hardest.

What happened: slate A's extra candidates made every rank worth 2 more points, on every ballot. Slate B has 3 voters to slate A's 2, so each step up adds 3 points to B2 but only 2 to A1. B2 started 1 point behind A1 and ended 1 point ahead. With short ballots, more candidates in the race help the bigger slate.

Simulation 2 · Disciplined slates, each voter ranks 550 candidates

With short ballots, the more candidates slate A runs, the fewer seats it wins

Under Borda, slate A wins 0.8 seats with 1 candidate and nothing once it runs 8. Under Borda, slate A falls from 8 seats with 10 candidates to under 1 seat with 100. Same voters as simulation 1. The only difference is how many candidates each voter ranks, and it flips the direction of the effect.

Simulation 3 · Loose slates, each voter ranks 550 candidates

With looser loyalty, Borda punishes a slate that runs too many

Here voters lean toward their own slate (4 times as likely to pick it at each rank) but often rank the other slate too, closer to how members vote in real organizations. Slate A does best running 3 or 4 candidates under either method. Past that, Borda drops it below 1 seat, while STV slips only to 1.4. Slate A does best running 30 to 40 candidates under either method. Past that, Borda drops it from 22 seats to 9, while STV slips only to 21.

Playing it out · Perfectly loyal slates choosing a strategy

Under Borda, ranking order and slate size become the strategy

Every voter is perfectly loyal and ranks only their own slate, with as many slots as seats. The majority runs one candidate per seat. The minority picks how many candidates to run, and always ranks them in the same order, which is its best choice. The majority picks one of two orders:

  • Fixed order: every majority voter ranks the slate in the same order the slate hands out.
  • Rotated order: the majority splits its voters into groups that each rank the slate in a shifted order, so every candidate ends up with the same points.
Majority uses a fixed order Majority rotates its order Minority's fair share

Seats are worked out exactly from the points, not simulated. When candidates tie for the last seat, the chart splits the seat between them, as a draw by lot would on average. Running more names than there are seats only hurts the minority further, so the chart stops at one per seat.

What it means

Under Borda, a slate's seats depend on a choice that has nothing to do with what voters want: how many names it puts on the ballot. Whether more names help or hurt depends on how many candidates voters rank. And when voters follow their slate exactly, the order a slate tells its voters to rank in can matter more than the votes: a majority that rotates its order can take nearly every seat.

STV doesn't work by position points, so an extra candidate only matters if voters actually move their votes to them. A slate that badly over-runs still loses a little under STV (votes leak to the other slate as its candidates are eliminated), but nothing like the Borda swing.

What this shows and doesn't. The simulations use two slates and a simplified voter (each voter picks one candidate at a time, favoring their slate and popular candidates). They show how each counting method reacts to slate size. They don't predict a real election's numbers.

With 100 voters and 50 seats, a candidate needs only 2 votes to win under STV, so single votes move seats. The STV lines wobble a little for that reason.
