Rank Depth Tuner

Change how simulated voters behave, and see two things at once: how deep rankings matter in your election, and whether the simulated elections still look like real ones. Each setting shows where real elections sit on its scale, and what it does in your election.

What the turnout range does

Each simulated election draws its own turnout around the expected number: about 2 in 3 land within the range, and 19 in 20 within twice the range. You can't know turnout to the voter, and under Scottish rules a few voters can change the answer a lot, because the quota (the votes needed to win a seat) is a whole number of votes, and the answer rises and drops with every step in it. Spreading turnout across the range you expect gives one steady answer. Set it to how far turnout has varied between your recent elections; 10% is the default. 0% plays out one exact number of voters.

The model plays out your election this many times, each with different random voters. More runs give steadier numbers.

Ballot

Your organization's ballot says "rank up to as many candidates as there are seats".

Of the voters who would stop before N on their own, the share who keep going and rank exactly N because the ballot names that number.

Why change it

Real ballots give 23% for "rank up to N" (US city limits of 3 to 6; most fall between 14% and 36%) and 97% for "rank at least N" (Canberra). No real ballot has a limit near 46, so if your members follow the ballot's number more or less than that, try other values and see how much the results move.

Voters

Who is voting

Sets how many candidates voters rank on their own, before the ballot's rules. Members are fitted to 87 union and society elections and rank more as fields grow. Public voters are fitted to 1,100 Scottish council elections and rank about 3 of 7.

Why change it

Nobody yet knows which your members resemble. For a 75-candidate, 46-seat election with no limit, members would rank about 29 on average and public voters about 10. Switch between them to see how much that changes how deep rankings matter.

Slates

Slates are groups of candidates whose voters tend to rank each other: caucuses, tendencies, or parties.

Why change it

Set it to how many organized groups usually run in your elections. A typical Scottish ward has 4 to 6 parties standing. Earlier runs found the number of slates barely changed results.

A slate backed by 30% of voters can expect about 30% of the seats. With the first choice, it runs about that many candidates, as your organization's slates do, and as Scottish parties do. With the second, slates divide the whole field between them in proportion to their supporters, so they run longer lists and few candidates are left on no slate.

Why change it

To test whether running a list sized to expected seats helps a slate, compared with running a longer list. That's one of the questions this project can answer.

The smallest share of candidates who run on no slate, like independents. Candidates left over once slates have their lists also run on no slate.

Why change it

If more or fewer candidates in your elections run without a slate's backing.

How much more often a voter's second choice goes to another candidate on their own slate than popularity alone would give, in percentage points. Scottish voters whose first choice has a running mate pick that running mate second about 82% of the time, where popularity alone would give 21%: 61 points of loyalty.

Why change it

Set it to how tightly your slates' voters stick together. Loose, like endorsement slates, is the default; tight, like Scottish parties, is the other end. Every headline result should be checked at both ends. The scale is measured on simulated Scottish wards; your election's own figure is shown below the slider.

When a voter's next choice leaves their slate, how evenly those choices spread over the other slates' candidates. 1 means perfectly evenly; lower means voters of one slate favor particular other slates, like neighbors on the left or right.

The slider's scale is measured on simulated Scottish wards, where real ballots give a reference. In elections shaped differently, the same setting can measure differently, so check the figure for your election.

Why change it

If your slates have strong pairings, such as two caucuses whose voters back each other, lower it. Real Scottish wards measure 0.85, slightly more even than the model can make; the model's most even setting is about 0.83.

Candidate popularity

Each candidate gets a random amount of support, before slates come in. This sets how far apart those amounts are. At 0, every candidate is equally popular. At 0.7, a typical candidate is about twice as popular, or half as popular, as average; at 1.0, about 2.7 times.

Why change it

This setting moved earlier results more than any other. The fitted value makes the most popular candidate's lead over the quota (the share of votes that guarantees a seat) match real elections of the same type. Raise it for elections with one or two clear favorites; lower it for elections where support is spread evenly.

The share of candidates with almost no support, far behind everyone else. In most real Scottish wards, the last candidate gets under 3% of first choices.

Why change it

Raise it if your elections have several candidates with almost no chance. Compare "Last candidate's first choices" in the comparison below: if the model's last candidates are stronger than real ones, add long shots.

How much less support long shots get than they otherwise would. Only matters when there are long shots.

Why change it

To make long shots more or less hopeless. Below 0, long shots turn into standouts, far more popular than the rest: an option still under review, which fixed the weak last candidate in large organizational elections.

Random seed

The starting value for the random voters. The same seed and settings always give the same results; a different seed shows how much results vary by chance.

How deep rankings matter in your election

Each simulated election is counted with full ballots, then again with every ballot cut short after rank 1, after rank 2, and so on.

Elections that come out the same when ballots are cut short

For each rank, the share of simulated elections where cutting every ballot after that rank, or after any deeper rank, elects exactly the same people as full ballots. The line rises to 100% at the rank past which no cut matters in any election. The marked points show where it crosses 50%, 80%, and 95% of elections: the 50%, 80%, and 95% confidence ranks.

Elections where one cut changes the winners

For each rank, the share of elections where cutting every ballot after exactly that rank elects at least one different person. The dashed line is 5 in 100 elections; the marked point is where the curve drops below it for good.

Seats that change hands

For each rank, how many of the seats go to someone else when every ballot is cut after that rank: the average across elections, and the most in any one election. The marked points are where the average drops below 1 seat, and where no election changes any seat.

How turnout changes how many ranks matter

Plays out your election at a range of expected turnouts, from the lowest to the highest, with every other setting the same, including the turnout range around each one: about 2 in 3 of each point's elections have turnout within that range of the expected number. The turnout range here is the same setting as under Expected voters; changing either changes both. For each expected turnout, it plots the same number as the headline: the rank by which 95 of 100 elections are decided, and the same for 80 and 50 of 100. A line that rises means a bigger turnout needs voters to rank further. The shaded band, around the 95% line, is the range that chance alone could give with this many simulated elections: if two points fall inside each other's bands, the difference between them could be luck. It runs your election at about 10 evenly spaced turnouts, plus your expected one (a few seconds each at 1,000 runs), so it starts only when you ask.

Do the simulated elections look like real ones?

The model plays out a twin of each real election in a sample: the same candidates, seats, voters, and ballot rules, with your slate and popularity settings. Close means the typical twin is within about 10% of the typical real election.
