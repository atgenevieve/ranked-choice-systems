# How common are ranked elections that fill 50 or more seats at once?

Research gathered October 2026 for organizers who run a large ranked (STV) election and want to know whether it is unusual. Check each source directly before quoting it in public; some figures come from search summaries and secondary pages, and the uncertain ones are flagged.

## Bottom line

- **Filling 50 or more seats in a single ranked count is very unusual.** I found no public election anywhere, now or in the past, that fills 50 seats in one single transferable vote (STV) count. The largest current public example is the **Western Australian Legislative Council, which has filled 37 seats in one statewide count since 2025**. Next come the New South Wales Legislative Council (21 seats) and a few Irish and Indian examples (10 to 19 seats). Counts where legislators, not the public, were the voters went a little higher: Uttar Pradesh filled 31 Rajya Sabha seats in 1952, and the Irish Dáil filled 30 Seanad seats in one count in 1922.
- **Most public STV elections fill 3 to 7 seats per count.** Ireland, Malta, Scotland, Northern Ireland, Tasmania, the ACT and Victoria all sit in that range. Reform bodies and researchers often recommend that range too.
- **Bodies with 100 or more seats almost always do something else.** They split the electorate into smaller districts that each run their own count, or they use party lists, as in the Netherlands (150 seats) and Israel (120). Party-list voters pick a party, not a ranked list of people.
- **In member organizations, counts of 30 to 80 seats do happen, mostly for convention delegates and student councils.** The largest found is **DSA-LA, which filled 77 delegate seats in one Scottish STV count in 2021**, from 150 candidates and 593 voters. Metro DC DSA filled **46 delegate seats in one STV count** in 2025, from 75 candidates and 432 ballots. The University of Sydney's student council filled **39 seats in one count** in 2021. Organizational elections are poorly documented online, so larger counts may exist.
- **So your election is not unheard of, but it sits at the far end of what anyone does.** The count still works mathematically. What changes is the voter's experience: the ballot is long, and most voters rank only a handful of names. See [How voters behave on very long ballots](#how-voters-behave-on-very-long-ballots).

## Terms used here

- **STV (single transferable vote):** a way to fill several seats at once. Voters rank candidates. Any candidate who reaches a set number of votes (the **quota**) wins. Votes a winner doesn't need move on to each voter's next choice, and the last-place candidate is dropped and their votes move too, until every seat is filled. "Hare-Clark," "Meek," and "Scottish rules" are versions of STV that differ in how they move surplus votes.
- **District magnitude:** the number of seats filled by one count. A city that elects 9 councillors citywide in one count has a magnitude of 9. A city that elects 12 councillors as four separate 3-seat districts has a magnitude of 3, even though 12 people win.
- **Droop quota:** the usual winning threshold. It is the number of valid votes divided by (seats + 1), plus a little. With 46 seats, a candidate needs a bit over 1/47 of the votes, about 2%.
- **Block voting (vote for N):** each voter gets as many votes as there are seats, and the top vote-getters win. It is not proportional: a group with just over half the voters can win every seat.
- **Party-list proportional representation:** voters choose a party, and parties get seats in proportion to their votes. It handles very large bodies easily because voters make one choice, not dozens.

## Public elections using STV

### What is typical

Almost every public STV election in use today fills **3 to 7 seats per count**:

- **Ireland's Dáil** uses 3 to 5 seats per constituency. The constitution sets the minimum at 3, and ordinary law sets the maximum at 5. An Irish parliamentary committee recommended in 2010 that constituencies have at least 4 seats.
- **Malta's parliament** and the **Northern Ireland Assembly** use 5 seats per district.
- **Northern Ireland councils** use 80 areas of 5, 6 or 7 seats: 34, 30 and 16 areas respectively ([ARK](https://ark.ac.uk/elections/flg14.htm)).
- **Scottish councils** use wards of mostly 3 or 4 seats. Wales's 2021 law, which lets Welsh councils choose STV, requires 3 to 6 seats per ward ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/asc/2021/1/section/12?view=plain)).
- **Tasmania** went from 5 to 7 seats per division in 2024. The **ACT** uses 5 per electorate.
- **US cities** that use STV fill even fewer seats per count: Cambridge 9, Portland 3 per district, and Albany and the Arlington primary 2.

### The largest public STV counts

1. **Western Australia Legislative Council, 37 seats (2025 onward).** The 2021 electoral reform law merged six regions into one statewide count. The 2025 ballot had 146 candidates in 13 groups, and the quota was about 2.6% of the vote, roughly 41,000 votes.
2. **New South Wales Legislative Council, 21 seats.** Half the 42-member chamber is elected statewide at each election. In 1999, 264 candidates stood. The ballot paper was about one metre wide and earned the nickname "the Tablecloth Election." It led to reforms that made such ballots smaller ([NSW Parliament](https://parliament.nsw.gov.au/about/Pages/Unlocking-the-House-exhibition-The-1999-Tablecloth-Election.aspx)).
3. **Irish Seanad, 1925, 19 seats.** The whole country was one 19-seat constituency, with 76 candidates and 23% turnout. It was the only time the public elected the Seanad directly, and it was not repeated.
4. **New York City Council, 1937 to 1945.** Each borough elected as many members as it had blocks of 75,000 votes, so seat totals rose and fell with turnout. The council had 26 seats in 1937 and 17 in 1943.
5. **Australian Senate.** A normal election fills 6 seats per state, and a double dissolution (a full Senate election called to break a deadlock) fills 12. The 2016 New South Wales double-dissolution ballot had 151 candidates.
6. **Counts where legislators were the voters.** In 1922 the Irish Dáil elected 30 of the first 60 senators "as a single 30-member contest" by STV: 112 candidates, 81 voters, 35 counts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1922_Seanad_election)). In 1952 Uttar Pradesh's legislators filled the state's 31 Rajya Sabha seats ([original Fourth Schedule](https://www.constitutionofindia.net/schedules/allocation-of-seats-in-the-council-of-states)). The constitution requires STV, and terms were split into retirement groups only after that first election, which points to one 31-seat count. No published count sheet was found, so treat this one as likely, not confirmed. Madras (27) and Bihar (21) were in the same position.
7. **Others in the 10 to 14 range:** the Irish Seanad's Agricultural and Labour panels (11 each, voted on by about 1,000 politicians), South Australia's Legislative Council (11), India's Rajya Sabha (Uttar Pradesh fills 10 at once, voted on by 403 state legislators), Dunedin City Council (14 at-large), and Birkirkara's local council in Malta (13).

**No public STV election I found has filled 50 seats in one count.**

As background knowledge, not re-verified here: Thomas Hare, who invented STV in 1859, proposed electing the whole UK House of Commons as one national constituency of more than 600 seats. No country adopted that idea.

### Big proportional elections use party lists, not ranked ballots

The largest single-count elections in the world use party-list proportional representation. The Netherlands elects 150 members, and Israel 120, in one nationwide count. Voters pick a party, and the party's own ordered list decides who fills its seats. Voters don't rank individual candidates, so ballot length never becomes a problem.

Japan's upper house once elected 50 members at a time from one nationwide district, but each voter cast **one** vote for one candidate, with no ranking and no transfers ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Councillors_national_district)).

## Large ranked elections in organizations

Organizations are where your election finds company. They are also much harder to research, because they rarely publish results.

### Examples near or above 50 seats

- **Metro DC DSA, 2025:** 46 national convention delegates elected by STV in one count. There were 75 candidates on three slates plus independents, and 432 valid ballots. The result split 21 / 14 / 6 / 1 / 4 across slates and independents ([Washington Socialist](https://washingtonsocialist.mdcdsa.org/ws-articles/2025-03-mdc-eclectic-delegation)). With 432 ballots and 46 seats, the quota is only about 10 votes.
- **DSA-LA, 2021: 77 delegates in one count, the largest organizational STV count found.** The chapter used Scottish STV on OpaVote. 150 candidates ran, 593 members voted, and the count took 109 rounds. The 15 alternates were the runners-up outside the top 77 ([DSA-LA](https://dsa-la.org/results-2021-national-convention-delegate-elections/)). With 593 ballots and 77 seats, the quota is about 8 votes.
- **NYC-DSA, 2021:** about 130 delegates, the largest DSA delegation ([Tempest](https://tempestmag.org/2021/06/dsa-chapter-delegate-results/)). In 2025 the chapter had 151 delegates, all but 5 elected on formal slates ([Socialist Majority](https://www.socialistmajority.com/theagitator/caucuses-resolutions-and-the-2025-convention), a caucus newsletter). How they were elected, in one count or by branch, was not found.
- **NYC-DSA:** a development page for the next convention says there will be "hundreds of delegates," divided among seven geographic branches. Each branch elects its own delegates on an electronic ranked ballot. That is the split-into-districts approach described later.
- **University of Sydney Students' Representative Council, 2021:** 39 councillors in one count. The first 17 winners reached the 128-vote quota, and the rest were filled as votes transferred. The council's size is set at one councillor per 1,000 undergraduates.

### Context from DSA

DSA chapters have used many methods for delegate elections: STV, Borda count (a points system where higher ranks earn more points), "approval-STV" hybrids, and plain block voting. Several of these results were challenged at the convention:

- East Bay DSA elected 38 delegates by Borda count in 2019, and members challenged the result, arguing that STV should have been used.
- The 2025 convention reportedly adopted resolution CB04, "Fair Representation via STV Delegate Elections," which requires STV for future delegate elections. This comes from caucus publications, not an official DSA document, so confirm the adopted text ([Red Star Caucus](https://redstarcaucus.org/convention), [Washington Socialist](https://washingtonsocialist.mdcdsa.org/ws-articles/25-10-dsa-con2025-red-slate)).

**Inference:** if that rule stands, large DSA chapters will regularly run STV counts of 30 to 100 or more delegates. That would make DSA one of the few places where very large single counts are routine.

### Typical organizational STV

Most organizations that use STV fill far fewer seats per count:

- Party committees: Liberal Democrats 12 to 15; Labour NEC constituency section 9.
- Union executives: UCU up to about 7 per contest.
- Boards: the Electoral Reform Society 12; Wikimedia 4.
- Church bodies: Church of England dioceses elect a few lay members each to General Synod, about 250 nationally.

Large bodies such as General Synod and the Co-op Group's 100-member council are filled through many small regional or diocesan counts, not one big one.

Counting methods vary. Meek's method is common where counting is done by computer, for example at Wikimedia and the Royal Statistical Society. UK public and union elections often use Scottish-style or ERS rules.

### Gaps in the evidence

- I found no union, professional society, cooperative or church body that fills 50 or more seats in a single published STV count.
- I could not confirm the counting method for DSA-LA in 2021, for NYC-DSA, or for the Co-op Group.
- Seat counts for the largest Church of England dioceses, such as London and Oxford, were not found. They are listed in General Synod paper GS 2162.

## What large bodies usually do instead

1. **Split into smaller districts.** This is by far the most common approach. Ireland (174 seats), Northern Ireland, Scotland, Tasmania, the ACT, Victoria and the Church of England all fill large bodies through many counts of 3 to 7 seats. NYC-DSA does the same with branches.
2. **Use party lists.** Used in the Netherlands, Israel, and most of continental Europe. Voters make one choice, so ballot length is not a problem.
3. **Use block voting ("vote for N").** Common in unions and associations and historically in the Australian Senate. It is simple, but a majority group can take every seat. UK Labour's NEC used it until 2020.
4. **Use slates or tickets.** Australian upper houses long let voters mark one box to adopt a party's full ranking (group voting tickets). NSW abolished these after 1999, the Senate in 2016, Western Australia in 2021 (effective 2025), and Victoria in 2026.

### Advice on district magnitude

No source sets a hard maximum, but the advice clusters between 3 and 7 seats:

- **Carey and Hix (2011)**, using 609 elections in 81 countries, found a "sweet spot" of about **4 to 8 seats** per district. Districts that size get most of the fairness benefits of proportional representation while keeping party systems simpler ([LSE](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/30631), [CISE](https://cise.luiss.it/2011/04/21/the-electoral-sweet-spot-low-magnitude-proportional-electoral-systems/)). Note that this study is about party systems and governments, mostly under list PR. It doesn't address ballot length.
- **The Electoral Reform Society** told Guernsey that with "any more than seven seats to fill," choosing among the candidates "would once again become a laborious process" ([ERS](https://electoral-reform.org.uk/?p=5057)).
- **The British Columbia Citizens' Assembly** (2004) preferred at least 5 seats for proportionality but proposed 2 to 7 per district ([BC Citizens' Assembly FAQ](https://citizensassembly.arts.ubc.ca/public/extra/bc-stv_faq_process.xml.htm)).
- **The Richard Commission** (Wales, 2004) proposed 4 to 6 seats, or 3 as an exception ([Senedd submission](https://business.senedd.wales/documents/s99925/ESB%2001%20Professor%20Denis%20Mollison.pdf)).
- **The Jenkins Commission** (UK, 1998) warned that a very long STV ballot "could offer a degree of choice which might be deemed oppressive rather than liberating" (quoted in [Lib Dem Voice](https://www.libdemvoice.org/tag/jenkins-commission)).

**Inference:** this advice is written for public elections with large, low-information electorates. A membership organization whose voters know many of the candidates, or vote by slate, may reasonably tolerate larger counts. The cost is that each voter's ballot covers only a small part of the field.

## How voters behave on very long ballots

The clearest pattern across the evidence: **voters rank about as many candidates as the instructions ask for, and rarely many more.** On long ballots, most voters use shortcuts such as party boxes or slates, or stop early.

- **Australian Senate before 2016:** voting below the line (ranking individual candidates rather than ticking one party box) required numbering every box. In 2013 that meant up to 110 candidates in NSW. About 95 to 97% of voters ticked a single party box instead. The AEC found that the number of candidates explained over half the variation in party-box voting rates from 1984 to 2013 ([AEC](https://www.aec.gov.au/About_AEC/research/files/sbps-atl-and-btl-voting.pdf)).
- **Australian Senate in 2016:** after the reform, voters were told to number at least 6 party boxes or at least 12 candidates.
  - **81.6% of voters numbered exactly 6 party boxes.** Only 0.8% numbered more than 12. Below-the-line voting rose to just 6.5% nationally, though it reached 28% in Tasmania, where voters know STV from state elections.
  - In NSW, with 151 candidates, only 5.4% voted below the line (Antony Green, [*Double Disillusion*, ch. 8](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n4149/pdf/ch08.pdf)).
  - The AEC also found that the more options on the ballot, the more ballots ran out of choices before the count ended. Nationally, 17% of the voting weight of below-the-line ballots ran out (exhausted), against 7% of party-box ballots. Across states, the correlation between number of options and exhaustion was 0.94 ([AEC exhaustion study](https://www.aec.gov.au/about_aec/research/files/sbps-exhaustion.pdf)).
- **ACT (5 seats per electorate):** about 98% of voters marked at least 5 preferences, as instructed. About 68% marked exactly 5, and about 30% marked more. An ACT Parliament paper found that the extra preferences did not materially change how the count played out ([ACT Parliament paper](https://www.parliament.act.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0009/3052467/Ballot-paper-preference-analysis-impact-of-ballot-paper-instructions.pdf)). This comes from a search summary because the PDF blocked direct access.
- **NSW Legislative Council (21 seats):** voters must number at least 15 candidates to vote below the line. In 2015, only about 2 to 3% did so, and most voters put a single "1" above the line. Sources give different figures, so treat these as approximate.
- **Scottish councils (3 to 4 seats):** in 2017, 86% of ballots showed more than one preference and 61% showed three or more, according to John Curtice ([ERS, *The Power of Preferences*](https://www.electoral-reform.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/The-Power-of-Preferences_STV-in-Scottish-Local-Elections.pdf)).
- **Cambridge, Massachusetts (9 seats):** I found no published median number of rankings. FairVote reports that voters follow informal slates heavily. For example, 503 voters ranked all nine candidates on one slate before anyone else.
- **Portland, Oregon (3 seats, up to 6 rankings):** about 91% of council voters ranked more than one candidate. In District 1, 21% ranked no council candidate at all ([FairVote](https://fairvote.org/ranked-choice-voting-debuts-in-portland-vast-majority-ranked-their-ballots-and-elected-a-candidate-of-choice/)).

**Inference for a 50-seat election:** expect most voters to rank somewhere between a handful of candidates and their slate's full list, not all 50 or more. Expect the ballot instructions to anchor how many they rank. Many ballots will run out of choices before the final seats are decided. That doesn't necessarily mean those votes were wasted. In Scottish data, most ballots that ran out had already helped elect someone; see McCune and Naber in [`literature.md`](literature.md). Measuring how much those deep ranks matter is the question this project exists to answer.
